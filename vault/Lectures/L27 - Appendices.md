## Chapter Content Walkthrough

- Help the client to execute their decision.
- Prepare and render a final account.
- Store the file in your firm's archives after telling your client where the papers will be kept and for how long.
An advocate has to take the following steps:

- Study the judgment carefully, for the reasons given earlier.
- Write up the brief.
- Prepare a short memorandum, not to be charged to the client, explaining the practical effect of the judgment to the client, and if there appears to be good reason to do so, comment on the correctness of the judgment.
- If briefed to do so, advise and counsel the client.
- Render a fee note.
- Return the brief with all the papers.
- Write up your legal research notes and file them away in the system you have created for the storage of your research materials and precedents.

A prosecutor has to note the verdict and sentence (in the event of a conviction) in the appropriate space on the docket cover and has to take the following additional formalities and administrative procedures in hand:

- Ensure that the docket is returned to the investigating officer in good order. If any original documents such as statements and the like have been removed from the docket to be handed in as exhibits, ensure that copies are left in their place(s) in the docket.
- Ensure that the court makes appropriate orders with regard to the exhibits. Certain exhibits will have to be destroyed. Others will have to be returned to the complainant.
- If licences are suspended or cancelled by the order of the court, ensure that the documents concerned are seized and endorsed and that the authority concerned be notified of the court's order.
- In some cases a death certificate may be issued on the strength of the court's findings as to the cause of death of the deceased. Ensure that the administrative procedures that are necessary are implemented.
- If the investigating officer has performed exceptionally well, write a minute to that effect to his or her commanding officer. Have the minute vetted and authorised by your superiors before you post or deliver it. Don't be patronising but give praise where praise is due.
- If anything is to be learned from the case, discuss the issue with the investigating officer so that both of you may improve your respective skills and techniques.

## 2 Trial notebook: *S v Graham*

### 2.1 Material fact(s) in issue

The material facts alleged in the charge sheet (the legal requirements or elements of the charge) are -

1. the accused (is the person who)
2. unlawfully
3. stole
4. a backpack (to the value of R150.00)
5. the property or in the lawful possession of Three Rings Sports (on 12/12/[year] at [town or city]).

Theft (or stealing) is defined in *S v Sibiya* 1955 (4) SA 427 (A) as follows:

Theft is committed when a person fraudulently and without claim of right made in good faith takes or converts to his use anything capable of being stolen with intent to deprive the owner thereof of his ownership (or any person having a special property or interest therein of such property or interest).

The *mens rea* element in theft (*animus furandi*) is complex and includes knowledge of the unlawfulness of the act. Its essence is that the accused must have taken the backpack concerned with the intent to deprive its owner permanently of it, and must have done so knowing that he was not entitled to take the backpack.

The material facts set out in the charge sheet can be restated as follows, having regard of the definition in *Sibiya*:

1. the accused
2. unlawfully
3. *animo furandi* (fraudulently and without claim of right made in good faith and with intent to deprive the owner permanently thereof)
4. took
5. a backpack (to the value of R150.00)
6. the property or in the lawful possession of Three Rings Sports (on 12/12/[year] at [town or city]).

The accused has admitted taking the backpack, its ownership and its value (in the section 115 statement). The issues as they appear from the charge sheet and the section 115 statement, are therefore whether the accused acted unlawfully and whether he had the requisite *mens rea*.

Unlawfulness cannot seriously be disputed and would have to be conceded at the trial. The accused clearly acted without consent and none of the other defences that would render his actions lawful is present at a *prima facie* level.

The sole issue is therefore whether the accused acted with requisite *mens rea* for theft, in particular, whether the accused knew that he was acting unlawfully.

## 2.4 Theory of the case

(1) The issue is going to be whether the accused had the necessary *mens rea* for theft.
(2) The accused's taking of the backpack without paying for it was the result of an understandable mistake on his part, rather than the intention to steal.
(3) The accused suffers from colour-blindness. He needed a bag as his backpack was old and torn. He wanted to make a good impression at a job interview. He needed both hands to examine the store's backpacks, so he put his own backpack on the floor. He walked from the backpack rack to the rack where bags were displayed, inadvertently carrying the store's backpack. He did not notice any difference in weight, texture or colour. His colour-blindness is of a type that makes a distinction between browns and reds difficult. He then left the store without paying, still thinking he was carrying his own backpack. When he realized his mistake, he immediately apologised. He had no intention to steal the backpack.
(4) The prosecution may contend that it was all a pretence; that he intended to steal.
(5) The problem with that suggestion is that there is no reason to reject the accused's version - the burden rests upon the State to prove that his version is false, beyond reasonable doubt.

## Draft opening statement

This is a case of an unfortunate but understandable mistake rather than deliberate wrongdoing.

The accused suffers from colour-blindness and by mistake took the store's backpack thinking it was his own.

The issue is therefore the *mens rea* element of theft.

Mr Graham cannot distinguish between colours in the brown and red range.

He went to the store to buy a new bag as his old backpack was torn. He had a newspaper in the backpack. He had kept it because it contained job advertisements he intended to follow up.

He wanted a new bag rather than a backpack, as he thought a bag might be more in keeping with the position he was seeking.

He looked at some backpacks. He put his own on the floor and mistakenly slung the store's backpack over his shoulder when he went to look at some bags.

When he found nothing to his liking, he left the store with the store's backpack still over his shoulder.
It was only when he was stopped outside that he realised he had the store's backpack and not his own.

He apologised for his mistake immediately but, unfortunately, it is the store's policy to prosecute in all cases.

I intend to call 2/3 witnesses.

The accused will give evidence as I have outlined already.

His family doctor, Dr Ivan Stone, will give evidence confirming that the accused is colour-blind, and will explain that the accused cannot distinguish between those colours normally sighted people see as reds and browns.

(Friend/schoolmate?)

The accused did not intend to take the store's backpack. He did not even realise he had it. He therefore lacked the necessary *mens rea* for theft.

## Draft closing argument

The issue is *animus furandi*.

The [[Page-92|burden of proof]] is on the Prosecution. It has to prove that a mistake such as that explained by the accused and Dr Stone **did not occur**. The test is whether the accused's version can reasonably be true. (See *S v Luther en"n*

---

Ander 1962 (3) SA 506 (A).) The test is subjective, whether the accused believed what he did was not unlawful. (See S v Sam 1980 (4) SA 289 (T); S v Botes 1966 (3) SA 606 (O).)

This is a heavy burden; proof beyond reasonable doubt is required.

If the court finds that the accused honestly believed that he was carrying his own backpack, then he lacked animus furandi and must be acquitted. (See S v Botes, supra; S v Bhaya 1953 (3) SA 143 (N).) Bhaya is binding on this court.

The absence of a dishonest intention is a complete defence. (See R v Kinsella 1961 (3) SA 519 (C).)

The evidence for the Prosecution is entirely circumstantial.

The accused looked around "furtively" - this is disputed, he was looking for assistance from a shop-assistant.

He walked out without paying - he did not realise he had the store's backpack.

He apologised - this is in consonance with his own version of the events.

The evidence for the defence is compelling. It is direct and it is reliable.

Dr Stone confirms that the accused cannot distinguish clearly between browns and reds.

The store's backpack is red; the accused's own is rust brown.

The two backpacks are so similar in design, weight and texture that the accused could easily be fooled into thinking he was carrying his own backpack.

The accused apologised immediately and said it was a "mistake".

The accused had been embarrassed by his colour-blindness in the past.

He is not a dishonest person.

The accused's conduct was not so unreasonable that the conclusion can be drawn that he must have known that he had the store's backpack. (See S v Sam supra.)

The accused must therefore be acquitted.

The appeal process is technical from beginning to end. At every stage there are special rules, time limits, principles applying to appeals only and Practice Directives that could derail even the most meritorious appeal. It is not the function of a book on skills to recapitulate these procedural principles. It is best to read a book like Harms Civil Procedure in the Superior Courts LexisNexis and the rules and cases referred to there. Nevertheless, the procedural requirements of the appeal process will be referred to where they have a direct bearing on the skills concerned.

The court appealed from is usually referred to as the court a quo, meaning the court "from which". That court could be a Trial Court, the Motion Court, or a Full Bench. There are also various courts of appeal and various levels of appeal. We generally think of appeals as going to the provincial division of the High Court or to the Supreme Court of Appeal, but there are other courts of appeal. The Labour Court and the Land Claims Court have their own appeal structures. Even the Magistrates' Courts have appeal functions. There is also the Constitutional Court with its own unique functions. Generally the idea is [Page 448] that there should be one appeal only, but an appeal could also go from one level of court of appeal to an even higher court of appeal.

The procedures for these different courts and levels of appeal are similar but not identical. The differences have more to do with the escalating degrees of formality in procedure and in the increasing importance of the subject-matter the higher one goes in the hierarchy of appeal courts than with the underlying appeal procedures. For the purpose of demonstrating the skills required for all the appeal processes noted earlier, this chapter focuses on the skills and techniques required for the different stages of an appeal from the High Court to the Supreme Court of Appeal. Those skills and techniques can be applied to all other courts of appeal.

### 25.1.1 Judgment or order subject to appeal

Before taking any steps to note or advance an appeal, counsel has to consider whether the judgment or order is appealable and if so, whether leave to appeal is necessary. Other questions then arise. What is the test for success in an application for leave to appeal? To which court does the appeal lie? How does the test for success in the appeal itself differ from the test for leave to appeal?

The decision taken on appeal must amount to a "judgment or order" for it to be appealable. In this context "judgment" and "order" are synonyms. The effect rather than the form of the judgment must be looked at. In some cases the statute or rule concerned determines whether a judgment is appealable. For example, Rule 43 orders are not appealable because section 20(7) of the Supreme Court Act 59 of 1959 (referred to in this chapter as "the Act") provides that they are not. Section 48 of the Magistrates' Courts Act 32 of 1944 lists five categories of judgments of the Magistrates' Courts that can be taken on appeal.

Where there is no statutory prohibition regulating the issue, Harms (in an earlier edition) suggested a simple three-step test to determine whether a judgment is appealable:

☐ First, is the judgment final in the sense that it is not susceptible to alteration by the court who gave it?
☐ Second, is the judgment definitive of the parties' rights?
☐ Third, does the judgment dispose of at least a substantial portion of the relief claimed in the case?

If the answer to any of these questions is in the negative, the judgment amounts to no more than a "ruling" and is therefore not appealable. Harms relied on case law as authority and gives the following examples of judgments which are appealable and rulings which are not appealable:

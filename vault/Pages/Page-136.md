## 12.8 Assembling the evidence: The prosecutor's position

Prosecutors may adapt the processes described thus far to the needs of criminal cases. Generally speaking the assembling of the evidence in a criminal case will be done for the prosecutor by the investigating officer, acting under the guidance of the prosecutor. Unlike civil cases, criminal cases usually involve a number of court appearances. At each appearance the prosecutor will have - or should have - access to the docket in order to determine whether the investigation has been completed to his or her satisfaction and whether the case is ready for trial. When that stage has been reached the prosecutor should have a short meeting with the investigating officer before fixing the trial date.

[[[Page-136|page 224]]]

There are some processes that are unique to a criminal case and the guidance provided to the investigating officer during the litigation phase - which for a prosecutor starts at the first appearance - should include -

- [ ] the need for and conduct of an identity parade (section 37(1) of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977 ("the Act");
- [ ] the need for taking the fingerprints, palm-prints or foot-prints of the accused (section 37(1) of the Act);
- [ ] the need to have blood or tissue samples taken (section 37(2) of the Act);
- [ ] advice on any bail application, including suitable conditions for bail;
- [ ] the need for steps to be taken for the protection of any witness (under the Witness Protection Act 112 of 1998);
- [ ] the need to ask that the hearing or part of it should be conducted in camera, whether in terms of the Child Justice Act 75 of 2008 or any other act (section 153 of the Act);
- [ ] the need for evidence to be given through an intermediary (section 170A of the Act);
- [ ] the need for a prohibition against publication of certain information relating to the proceedings (section 154 of the Act);
- [ ] the need for a prohibition against publication of the identity of persons towards or in connection with whom certain offences have been committed (section 335A of the Act).

Plea and sentencing agreements were discussed in chapter 3. At the sentencing stage after conviction the prosecutor will have another opportunity to guide the investigating officer in the quest to obtain evidence that may be relevant to the sentence the court might or ought to impose. Ideally this kind of evidence should be sought during the original investigation of the matter and not be left until a conviction has been obtained. Evidence that may be relevant to sentence includes the accused's record of prior convictions, evidence of criminal conduct while out on bail pending the trial (showing that the accused is unrepentant), and evidence in support of a compensation order in favour of the complainant.

This is not a closed list and prosecutors should take advice and guidance from their seniors when deemed necessary.

> [!warning] 12.9 Protocol and ethics

- [ ] It is a good idea to set out the precise instructions received in the client's written statement. That way there can be no dispute later about the mandate. Even such relatively innocuous things like which witnesses to call, whether the accused will give evidence, how the accused will plead, and what offers to make to the other side should be recorded in writing.
- [ ] Be careful not to impose a personal view of the facts, or what is seen at the scene, on the witnesses.
- [ ] Take measurements, photographs and videos, if necessary, to avoid becoming a witness.
- [ ] Remember that an expert witness owes a duty to the court, first and foremost.

## 15.1 Introduction

The conduct of counsel (whether an attorney or an advocate) in and around the courtroom is regulated by certain protocols, the ethics of the legal profession and some old customs. To the casual observer, there may appear to be a bewildering array of strange rules according to which the game is played. Some lawyers walk around in robes. Others wear formal suits, even the women. The lawyers on opposing sides talk to each other in muted tones; they even have tea together. They say, "As it pleases the court" and, "With respect" every now and then. They half get out of their chairs every now and then and bow to the judge, muttering something like, "'s court pleases".

What are the protocols and rules of etiquette concerning counsel's conduct at court? Note the qualification; at court, not in court. The fact is that a certain standard of behaviour is expected of lawyers, both in and out of court. When lawyers are engaged in litigation, they will have to deal with judges, other lawyers, clients, witnesses, the court's staff and members of the public. There are extensive protocols and Rules of Ethics regulating a lawyer's conduct generally. The subject (of ethics) is so wide that it deserves a book of its own; the emphasis in this chapter is on the basic protocols and rules of etiquette of the courtroom a newly admitted practitioner needs to know in order to get by without too many mishaps.

## 15.2 Counsel's primary duty and essential rights

All lawyers are servants of the administration of justice. Every lawyer plays a role in it. In the litigation process we serve two interests, the court and the client. For prosecutors the client is the administration of justice. For defence counsel it is the accused in the dock. There was a time when it was said that the interests of the client were always paramount, but it is now accepted that counsel has a higher duty to the court. Generally speaking, counsel serves the administration of justice by representing the client in accordance with counsel's duty to the court.

It is fundamental to the litigation process as a means of administering justice that everyone should be represented by a competent lawyer of his or her choice. Contrary to popular belief, lawyers actually help the courts to arrive at correct decisions. We help by [[[Page-164|page 274]]] taking adversarial positions and by conducting the litigation according to the rules; thus we help the court to arrive at a decision which is in accordance with the proven facts and the law. We serve the administration of justice by rendering a competent, professional and ethical service to the client.

Counsel therefore has the right and duty to present the client's case fully and properly so that it can be seen in the best possible light, no matter who the client is or what the client is alleged to have done. Arising from this right and duty, counsel has the right and the obligation to insist on an opportunity to present the client's case at every stage of the proceedings. Anything that detracts from counsel's right to present the case fully is wrong. A lawyer who does not stand on their right to be given the fullest opportunity to present the client's case when an obstacle is placed in his or her way, is guilty of a dereliction of duty. On the one hand, counsel is entitled to do his or her duty without being deterred by any fear of displeasure, ridicule or punishment. On the other hand, counsel is obliged to do his or her duty in spite of any fear of displeasure, ridicule or punishment.

Counsel's duty to the court requires them -:

- [ ] not to advise the client to pursue litigation unless there is a reasonable case to be made;
- [ ] not to deceive the court knowingly or recklessly on the facts or the law;
- [ ] not to tender evidence known (by counsel) to be false;
- [ ] not to present argument or points which are obviously specious or frivolous; and
- [ ] not to withhold contrary authorities from the court.

Counsel's duty to the client requires them -

- [ ] to give objective, practical advice;
- [ ] to provide advocacy which is competent, fearless, ethical and professional; and
- [ ] to give effect to the client's legitimate instructions.

Prosecutors have an additional duty. They have to be fair, for example, by not withholding evidence they know will exonerate the accused.

The courts, the clients and the general public are entitled to expect counsel to perform their duty with meticulous care. The duty to the court may sometimes come into conflict with the duty to the client. In such a case, the duty to the court takes precedence. Counsel must (not may) refuse to act (or to continue acting) for a client who refuses to allow counsel to do his or her duty to the court, for example, by bringing adverse authority to the notice of the court. The duty to the court does not require defence counsel to produce evidence against the client's interests, even if the availability of such evidence is known to counsel. It is perfectly permissible to require the other side to prove their case to the required standard. One could put it as follows: In matters of law it is counsel's duty "to lead the court". In matters of fact, it is counsel's duty "not to mislead the court". Subject to a prosecutor's duty to be fair, this is also true for prosecutors.
